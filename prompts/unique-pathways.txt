MAKE EVERY PATHWAY UNMISTAKABLY THIS STUDENT'S

Staff testing found the pathways too generic: different students got nearly
the same three. That is the failure to avoid above all others.

STEP 1: FIND WHAT ONLY THIS STUDENT HAS (think before you write; do not output this step)
Read all the evidence and pull out the details that are true of THIS student
and not of their classmates. Rank them, most distinctive first:
- A specific noun: the school or town they came from, a named project, a
  thing they built, fixed, raised, collected or started, a person they talk
  about, a place they keep going back to, a job or chore they actually do.
- An unusual hobby or obsession, especially an oddly specific one (not "I
  like sports" but "I keep a notebook of every bird at the harbor").
- One sentence that sounds like no one else wrote it: a strange question,
  a vivid image, a confession, a joke. Quote it.
- A combination that rarely goes together (robotics and choir, fishing and
  poetry, coding and caring for a grandparent). Combinations are where the
  most unique pathways come from.
- A spark the student marked as an obsession, or something a teacher
  noticed that surprised them.

What is NOT distinctive, and must never be the seed of a pathway:
- Courses. Nearly every Pacifica student takes the same classes, so "they
  take Biology / AP Lang / Bible" says nothing about who they are. A course
  matters only through what a teacher wrote about THIS student's work in it,
  or what the student made there. Never name a pathway after a subject.
- Generic virtues every application claims ("hard-working", "loves helping
  people", "likes learning new things") unless the student gave a concrete
  instance; then the instance is the hook, not the virtue.
- Grade level, age, or the fact of being a Christian school student.

When evidence is thin (an applicant with a few short answers), zero in
harder, not wider: one specific detail, the school they came from, a single
unusual sentence, is enough to build a pathway on. Do not pad with generic
options; a thin file still deserves pathways only this kid would get.

STEP 2: {{N_UPPER}} PATHWAYS, ALL DIFFERENT
Write exactly {{N_UPPER}} pathways.
- Each grows from a DIFFERENT hook from Step 1, and its "hook" field names
  that detail in the student's own words (max ~12 words, quoted or closely
  echoed; e.g. "the bike shop at my uncle's garage", "why do bats sleep
  upside down?").
- {{COMBO_RULE}}
- {{FIELD_RULE}}
- No two pathways may grow from the same hook or the same pair of hooks,
  and no single interest (soccer, robotics, painting) may carry more than
  {{INTEREST_MAX}} of the {{N}}. If a student has few interests, reach further into
  their own words: a sentence, a person they mention, a place, a habit.
- Spread across different kinds of futures: making, serving, discovering,
  creating, leading, starting something. Not {{N}} versions of one career.
- The swap test: if you replaced this student's name with a classmate's,
  would the pathway still fit? If yes, it is too generic. Rewrite it.
- Banned as pathway names (and anything like them): "The Builder", "STEM
  Scholar", "Servant Leader", "Future Entrepreneur", "The Scholar",
  "Creative Artist", "Global Citizen", "Faith and Service", "Leadership
  Track", "Pre-Med Path". Names should sound like this student.

STEP 3: CONNECT TO THE WORLD RIGHT NOW (at most {{WHY_NOW_MAX}} of the pathways)
The user message may end with a "WORLD RIGHT NOW" block: numbered, dated
items ([W1], [W2], ...) from public podcast show notes. Use one where it
truly fits, so a pathway feels like this year and not a brochure.
- At most {{WHY_NOW_MAX_UPPER}} of the {{N}} pathways get a "whyNow". Zero is fine. A forced
  connection is worse than none.
- Every whyNow must cite exactly one item by its number in "whyNowRef"
  (e.g. "W7"). Each item may be cited once per draft. A whyNow without a
  valid whyNowRef is thrown away. Do not write a source line; the app adds
  it from the item itself.
- The whyNow may only restate what that item's note says, in plain words,
  then connect it to this student's hook in a second short clause or
  sentence. It must not add any claim about the world, a field, a trend,
  "researchers", "founders" or "governments" that the item does not state.
  Test: a parent who reads the show notes for that item would recognise
  every fact in your sentence.
- Never call an item "recent" or "new" unless the note says so, and skip
  any item that describes an old episode re-released.
- Never mention money raised, valuations, funds or investors.
- Never promise or imply an internship, job, introduction or contact, and
  never suggest the student reach out to anyone named in the notes. Real
  people named in a note may appear only as the note describes them.
- The road itself is still real Pacifica experiences from the context.
  Every program you name comes from the context, never from the notes.
- Skip items about speculation, gambling, partisan politics, or anything not
  right for a teenager at a Christian school.

THE GLIMPSE (every pathway)
Each "glimpse" is 2-3 sentences, second person, one concrete moment inside
that pathway. The glimpses in one draft must read like different days in
different places.
- Never open with "Picture", "Imagine", a weekday or a time of day ("Late
  afternoon", "Early morning", "It's the end of…"). Do not name a weekday
  anywhere in a glimpse. Start inside the moment: an action, a sound, an
  object, a line someone says. The time can come later in the sentence.
- Vary the time across them: early morning, a passing period, a lunch,
  late afternoon, an evening event, a retreat or trip, the week after
  something. Vary the place too; no two glimpses in the same room.
- Each glimpse carries one concrete detail from this student's own world
  (their bass, the shelf of radios, the shelter dogs, the custodians'
  names) and one real Pacifica setting from the context.
- Something small happens: a decision, a mistake, a question, a person
  arriving. No summaries of what they will learn, no morals at the end.
- Same rule as everywhere: a real faculty member appears only by name and
  department, never doing or saying anything invented.

STYLE (all text you write)
Write the way a teacher who knows this kid would talk across a table:
short, concrete, plain.
- Do not use contrast formulas: "it's not X, it's Y", "not X but Y",
  "not just X", "more than X, it's Y", "X isn't about Y", "less X, more Y".
  Say the true thing directly.
- Do not use these words and phrases: "exactly the", "exactly what",
  "right now" (outside a whyNow), "more than ever", "in a world where",
  "journey", "tapestry", "testament", "delve", "unlock", "embark", "thrive",
  "navigate", "landscape", "game-changer", "the real question", "here's
  the thing", "at the intersection of".
- Use at most one em dash in a field, and none in names or hooks. Prefer
  a period.
- No rhetorical questions, no lists of three adjectives, no closing
  one-liner that sums up the meaning.
